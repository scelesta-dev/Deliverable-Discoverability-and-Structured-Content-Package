Dial It In

Understanding camera settings allows photographers to take greater creative control over their images. ISO, aperture, and shutter speed work together to influence image brightness, depth of field, and motion. Learning how these settings interact can help you achieve your desired photographic results.

ISO and image noise

ISO is one of the three basic camera settings that photographers use to control image brightness (the other two being aperture and shutter speed). In digital photography ISO is the setting that controls how the camera processes the signal from the image sensor to produce an image of a certain brightness. The standard ISO values are 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600 and 3200, though most modern cameras have a much wider range.

Lower ISOs (e.g. 100 or 200) are ideal in well-lit situations and will give you photographs with more detail, smoother colors and less image noise. Higher ISOs are of course useful in low light (indoor events, night time, dark rooms etc) but will introduce more image noise in the form of grain and color variation.

Choosing the right ISO setting is all about balancing the available light, the image quality you're after and the result you're looking for. A landscape photographer shooting in bright daylight might go for a low ISO to preserve fine detail. A sports photographer shooting in the kind of indoor conditions you'd find in a gym or similar environment may well be forced to go to a higher ISO to get a fast enough shutter speed. Understanding how ISO works with aperture and shutter speed is key to capturing different lighting situations while still achieving the quality you want. Aperture and f-stops

An aperture is an adjustable opening within the lens that controls how much light reaches the sensor. The size of the aperture is expressed as an f-number (an f-stop). Common apertures are f/1.4, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8 and f/16. The lower the f-number, the wider the aperture and the more light that gets into the camera. The higher the f-number, the smaller the aperture and the less light that gets into the camera.

In addition to controlling how much light enters the camera, aperture is also a major factor in determining depth of field. Depth of field is the distance between the nearest and the furthest point in a scene that appears acceptably sharp. A wide aperture (say f/1.8 or f/2.8) will generally give you a shallow depth of field, which is useful for isolating your subject from the background. A narrow aperture (say f/8 or f/11) will give you a much greater depth of field, and so more of your scene will be in focus.

Photographers choose their aperture settings according to the light and their creative intent. Portrait photographers will typically use a wide aperture to isolate their subject and create a soft background. Landscape and architectural photographers will more often use a narrow aperture. Aperture is all about controlling the amount of emphasis you give to different parts of an image, creating a sense of depth and determining the overall feel of the image. Shutter speed and motion

Shutter speed is the length of time that the camera's image sensor is exposed to light when taking a photograph. It's normally expressed in seconds or fractions of a second (e.g. 1/1000, 1/500, 1/250, 1/125, 1/60). The faster the shutter speed the less time the sensor is exposed to light, the slower the shutter speed the longer the sensor is exposed to light. It has a direct bearing on exposure and on the amount of movement that will be present in the image.

Fast shutter speeds are ideal for freezing action and capturing moving subjects with minimal motion blur. That's why sports, wildlife and action photographers are so often seen using fast shutter speeds to 'freeze' the action. Slow shutter speeds can be used to create motion blur in the image to indicate movement, for instance a waterfall or the light trails of a moving vehicle. This is a common technique in long exposure photography (especially at night).

The subject's movement obviously dictates shutter speed, the available light, and the photographer's intentions. With slower shutter speeds, you'll need a tripod or some form of image stabilization to avoid camera shake that causes blur. You also have to bear in mind how shutter speed works in conjunction with aperture and ISO to determine the overall brightness of your image. A good understanding of shutter speed gives you far greater control over how motion and time are rendered in your photograph.
